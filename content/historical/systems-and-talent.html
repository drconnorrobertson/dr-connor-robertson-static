People bring judgment, skill, and initiative to a business. Systems make the conditions for reliable work visible: the required inputs, the sequence, the acceptance standard, and the exceptions that need judgment. A strong operation needs both.

This updated edition retains the historical systems-versus-talent topic while replacing a false choice with a practical question: can a capable teammate reproduce the required result without depending on one person’s memory?

Identify the result that depends on a single person

Choose a recurring output, such as a project handoff, a service estimate, or a customer onboarding package. Ask which parts only one person can complete and why. The answer may be access, experience, an undocumented decision rule, or a relationship that has never been transferred.

Those causes need different responses. More documentation will not grant missing access. A detailed checklist will not replace specialist judgment. A new hire will struggle if nobody can describe what an acceptable result looks like.

Separate the repeatable path from the judgment

Write the common sequence with its prerequisites and checks. Mark the points where a person must interpret incomplete evidence, choose between conflicting priorities, or decide an exception. Give those decisions an owner and an escalation path instead of hiding them inside a long procedure.

  • Input standard: the information required before work begins.
  • Working sequence: the normal path that someone can follow.
  • Acceptance standard: the evidence that the output is ready for use.
  • Decision boundary: what the operator can decide and what must be escalated.
  • Learning loop: how an exception becomes a better instruction or a deliberate judgment case.

Run a transfer test

  1. Ask the experienced operator to demonstrate one ordinary case and explain the acceptance checks.
  2. Have another qualified teammate perform a comparable case using the draft procedure.
  3. Record clarification questions, missing inputs, wrong assumptions, and correction time.
  4. Update the procedure, repeat the test, and leave specialized decisions assigned to the appropriate reviewer.

The test is about whether the process supports good work. It is not a reason to judge someone’s capability from an unclear instruction or an unfamiliar system. Use an approved practice setting where mistakes can be corrected.

Worked example: the estimate only one person can prepare

Illustrative example: a senior estimator produces reliable estimates, while other team members omit required inputs. Observation shows that the senior estimator checks site conditions and service exclusions before opening the pricing worksheet. The team had documented the worksheet clicks but omitted the preparation decisions.

The improvement is a source checklist, an explicit exception rule, and an estimate review standard. Training then connects the procedure to real examples. Merely asking the rest of the team to follow the senior estimator more carefully would leave the missing decisions invisible.

Document authority as well as procedure

A teammate may know how to prepare an output but lack authority to accept a change or communicate a commitment. Record those limits. Use automation after the transfer problem is understood

An assistant can help turn observed notes into a first procedure draft. A person must then test it, correct the missing cases, and verify the final output. Use the SOP drafting workflow for that preparation step.
