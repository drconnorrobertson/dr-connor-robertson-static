A newly acquired business needs reporting that helps the incoming team choose its next action. A dashboard can provide that view, but only when the underlying definitions, source records, and decision owners are clear. Start with a short operating scorecard and improve it as the team learns the business.

This updated guide preserves the historical article’s focus on post-acquisition management dashboards. It adds a metric dictionary, a reconciliation routine, and an example of how to respond when a dashboard points to the wrong conclusion.

Choose the decisions before the charts

Write down the recurring decisions for the first month of ownership. The list might include which overdue work to escalate, whether the delivery backlog exceeds near-term capacity, and which customer issues need the new owner’s attention. A metric earns its place when someone can explain what decision it informs and what supporting record they will inspect.

Keep a separate view for learning the baseline and for monitoring current work. A historical trend assembled from inconsistent definitions can be useful as a question, but should not be presented as a clean comparison. Label any change in accounting treatment, service mix, or measurement period.

Build a metric dictionary the team can challenge

For each measure, record its definition, numerator and denominator where relevant, reporting period, source, owner, refresh schedule, and known limitations. Define completion in the same way across reports. A job marked complete by dispatch may still be awaiting customer acceptance or invoice review.

  • Work accepted: outputs that passed the delivery acceptance condition during the reporting period.
  • Open backlog: accepted work waiting for a defined delivery milestone, separated from unapproved proposals.
  • On-time completion: accepted work completed against the agreed due date, with rescheduled work identified.
  • Rework: time spent correcting a previously submitted output, including the reason for correction.
  • Unresolved customer issues: cases still awaiting an agreed resolution, with owner and next action.

Finance owns the definitions and reconciliation of financial measures. Link the scorecard to the approved reports rather than letting an AI summary create an alternative set of financial numbers.

Use a first-month reporting sequence

  1. Inventory existing reports. Identify the spreadsheets, system views, and informal updates people actually use. Ask where those reports disagree.
  2. Select a small decision set. Choose a handful of measures the team can maintain reliably. Keep unverified historical data clearly labeled.
  3. Reconcile a sample. Follow selected dashboard entries back to individual work records and then to the approved source totals.
  4. Run a weekly decision meeting. Discuss exceptions, assign owners, and record the next action. Change the dashboard when it fails to support that discussion.

Worked example: completed jobs are not accepted jobs

Illustrative example: the dispatch system shows 40 completed jobs, but five still lack required closeout evidence. Reporting 40 accepted jobs would hide the review queue. Show the 35 accepted jobs and five awaiting review as separate states, and verify the definitions with the delivery lead. The purpose is to expose the handoff, not blame the person entering the status.

Where AI can help

An assistant can draft commentary from approved figures, summarize open issues, or identify entries missing an owner. Require a source reference for each material statement. The person responsible for the scorecard checks the figures, period comparisons, and proposed interpretation before the team uses the brief.

Keep a record of decisions and definition changes

Save the meeting date, the exception discussed, the source checked, the action, and its owner. When a definition changes, record when the new definition became effective and whether older periods were restated. A stable decision log makes the dashboard useful to the next manager.
