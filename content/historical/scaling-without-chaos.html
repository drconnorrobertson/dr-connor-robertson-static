Growth creates more arrivals into a process. If the process already has unclear handoffs, inconsistent acceptance rules, or no room for exceptions, extra demand makes those problems more visible. A useful scaling plan connects demand, delivery capacity, quality, and decision ownership before increasing volume.

This updated edition of the historical article adds a simple capacity test and a controlled expansion routine. The goal is to help an operating team decide what can grow next and what must be repaired first.

Map one delivery stream from promise to acceptance

Choose a service or product with a recognizable path: qualified request, accepted scope, preparation, delivery, review, and customer acceptance. Measure work in a unit that makes sense for that stream. Counting projects equally can mislead when some require a day of effort and others require a month.

Record where work waits as well as where people actively work on it. A full delivery calendar may coexist with a much larger queue waiting for client inputs. Distinguish those states before concluding that another delivery hire will solve the bottleneck.

Compare workload with available capacity

Estimate the effort required by accepted work due in the period, then compare it with the capacity genuinely available for that type of work. Include recurring meetings, planned absences, review responsibilities, and known constraints. Use a range when task effort is uncertain, and revisit the range after completed work provides better evidence.

Illustrative arithmetic: six jobs requiring about eight delivery hours each represent 48 hours of work. If the responsible team has 36 delivery hours available, 12 hours need a scheduling or capacity decision. This is a planning estimate, not proof that overtime or a new hire is the right answer.

Check the next constraint before changing the first

A faster sales handoff may create a review backlog. Additional technicians may create a dispatch bottleneck. More finished work may create an invoicing queue. Follow the work through the full delivery stream rather than improving one stage in isolation.

  • Demand: what work has actually been accepted, and what remains uncertain?
  • Delivery: who has the required skills, equipment, information, and available time?
  • Review: who can accept the finished work, and when?
  • Exceptions: what happens when scope changes or an input is missing?
  • Customer communication: who updates the promised date when the plan changes?

Expand in a bounded step

  1. Establish a baseline for completed work, waiting, rework, and customer acceptance.
  2. Choose one change, such as a revised handoff or a small increase in scheduled volume.
  3. Define what would cause the team to pause expansion: growing unreviewed work, repeated missed promises, or unresolved quality issues.
  4. Review the result on comparable work and record expand, revise, or stop.

A bounded change makes the cause of a problem easier to see. If demand, staffing, software, and service scope all change together, a better headline result may conceal a worse process.

Make decision rights visible

The team needs to know who can change a schedule, accept additional scope, approve a customer recovery, or stop work awaiting evidence. Put those decisions in the workflow rather than leaving them to the founder’s availability. See the owner independence systems playbook for the wider leadership problem.

Use AI where the inputs are stable

AI can help assemble a capacity meeting brief from approved figures, group documented delay reasons, or draft a source-linked handoff. It should expose missing information and leave schedule commitments with the authorized operator. Count reviewer time when evaluating the pilot.
